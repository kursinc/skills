Develop before editing:

**harvest → choose → define → map → sequence → stress-test → brief**

The purpose is to discover the strongest essay latent in the material and give a downstream editor a stable argument contract. The default output is an editorial development brief, not finished prose.

Read any supplied voice guide, audience context, product context, and editorial constraints before beginning. Use [`DIAGNOSTICS.md`](DIAGNOSTICS.md) when a step exposes competing essays, unclear agency, weak warrants, product-led reasoning, or unstable causality. The intellectual foundations are summarised in [`FOUNDATIONS.md`](FOUNDATIONS.md); synthesise the methods rather than imitating any source.

## 1. Harvest the material

Extract every consequential item without preserving the draft's order. Classify each as:

- claim;
- tension or assumption;
- reason;
- evidence;
- example;
- warrant;
- qualification;
- causal proposition;
- consequence;
- adjacent possible essay.

Mark repeated items and distinguish true observations from material needed to earn the essay's argument.

**Complete when:** every substantive passage has one primary role, repeated intellectual work is visible, and every plausible governing claim has been captured.

## 2. Choose the editorial centre of gravity

Group the harvested material into competing essay candidates. For each candidate, state:

- the reader's familiar view;
- the complication;
- the proposed governing claim;
- the consequence for judgement;
- the material it would exclude.

Choose the candidate that produces the most distinctive, consequential, supportable change in reader judgement. Justify the choice against the strongest alternative.

**Complete when:** the essay can be described as one change in judgement rather than a list of topics, and the rejected alternatives have explicit destinations: subordinate reason, cut material, or separate essay.

## 3. Define the argument contract

State precisely:

1. intended reader;
2. reader problem;
3. governing claim;
4. governing tension;
5. commercial or practical standard;
6. consequence if the claim is accepted.

Build a claim–reason–evidence–warrant map. Separate symptoms from causes, examples from evidence, and qualifications required by truth from defensive framing.

**Complete when:** every principal reason has adequate support or is labelled as an evidential vulnerability, and every inferential step has a defensible warrant.

## 4. Extract governing distinctions

Compress repeated examples, consequences, and contrasts into the smallest useful vocabulary of distinctions. Each distinction must separate concepts the draft currently confuses and must explain several pieces of material.

Prefer five or fewer. Keep a sixth only when removing it creates a specific logical loss.

**Complete when:** the distinctions explain most retained examples, use stable terms, and do not duplicate one another at different levels of abstraction.

## 5. Map agency, responsibility, and capability

For every major action, decision, and consequence, identify:

- who acts;
- who has relevant evidence;
- who has authority;
- who carries cost or risk;
- what sits outside local control;
- where leadership responsibility lies;
- what technology enables;
- what technology cannot supply.

Attribute responsibility in proportion to information, authority, incentives, and control. Treat structural explanation as the default when competent local action can still produce an incoherent whole.

**Complete when:** no actor, team, leader, or platform is credited or blamed beyond the evidence, authority, or control available to it.

## 6. Build the conceptual hierarchy

Write one clause for the job of each proposed section. Arrange the sequence so each section creates the need for the next and every subordinate idea has a parent claim.

Assign examples only after the hierarchy is stable. Retain the strongest example for each necessary function; keep another only when it changes the mechanism, boundary, or consequence.

Define the technology or platform role in one sentence and retain only capabilities that support that role.

**Complete when:** the section jobs form one causal or logical chain, no section survives on topical relevance alone, and every retained example has a distinct job.

## 7. Stress-test the emerging essay

Run the relevant tests in [`DIAGNOSTICS.md`](DIAGNOSTICS.md). Challenge especially:

- unsupported warrants;
- category errors;
- contradictory standards;
- unclear causality;
- defensive framing;
- feature-led product logic;
- practitioner blame where structure explains more;
- repeated examples or consequences;
- conclusions that do not follow.

Paraphrase the governing claim and strongest formulations literally. Remove rhetorical symmetry and retest the proposition in plain language.

**Complete when:** the governing claim remains useful after its easiest overstatements are removed, and every unresolved weakness is named in the brief.

## 8. Produce the development brief

Return a structured brief containing:

- working title or title direction;
- intended reader;
- reader's familiar view;
- complication;
- governing claim;
- governing tension;
- commercial or practical standard;
- claim–reason–evidence–warrant map;
- governing distinctions;
- agency and responsibility map;
- technology or platform role;
- section sequence and the job of each section;
- examples to retain;
- examples and material to cut;
- evidential or logical vulnerabilities;
- rhetorical habits to ration;
- intended ending residue;
- explicit instructions for the downstream editing skill.

Add a short decision record naming the strongest rejected essay and why it lost.

**Complete when:** a downstream editor can draft or restructure the article without rediscovering its conceptual system, while retaining freedom over sentences, rhythm, and local expression.

## Output boundary

Return only the development brief unless the user explicitly asks for a finished article in the same request. When prose is requested, present the brief first and treat it as the binding input to the downstream edit.

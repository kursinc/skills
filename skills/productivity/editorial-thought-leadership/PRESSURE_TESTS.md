# Editorial pressure tests

Use these tests after the argument map. Apply the ones the draft triggers; do not turn the edit into a mechanical checklist in the final prose.

## The governing-argument test

Can the draft be reduced to one sentence without becoming vague?

If several sentences are required because each section makes a different point, the article may contain multiple essays. Choose the governing claim and subordinate the rest.

## The section-job test

What new work does this section perform?

A section earns its place by establishing a claim, changing the reader’s interpretation, introducing a necessary complication, or drawing a consequence. A section that merely supplies more proof of an accepted point should be cut or folded into the stronger section.

## The one-example test

Assume one example can carry a section.

Keep a second example only when it changes the reader’s understanding: a different mechanism, boundary, consequence, or type of conflict. A new industry, team, channel, or metric is not automatically a new idea.

After an example lands, move the reasoning forward.

## The catalogue test

Lists often conceal repetition.

A sequence of harms, departments, metrics, audiences, channels, or features may be several surface forms of one governing distinction. Replace the list with the distinction, then retain one or two items only when they make it concrete.

Prefer:

> The immediate metric may improve while the quality of trade deteriorates.

Over an exhaustive list of every way trade quality could deteriorate.

## The defensive-contrast test

Search for constructions such as:

- “the issue isn’t…”;
- “this isn’t an argument for…”;
- “the choice isn’t between…”;
- “the benefit isn’t…”;
- “it doesn’t replace…”;
- “the wrong conclusion is…”;
- “not X, but Y”.

These constructions are useful when they create a genuine turn. Across a long piece, they become a crutch that makes the argument sound permanently defensive.

State the positive claim directly wherever the reader no longer needs the boundary explained. Preserve contrast only when removing it would lose an important distinction.

## The local-to-commercial test

A local metric is evidence, not the final commercial judgement.

Ask what must remain true of the business as a whole: customer quality, trade quality, margin, trust, retention, cost, risk, or future behaviour. Select only the dimensions material to the argument; do not reproduce this list automatically.

The first standard is preservation to the best of the practitioner’s ability and available evidence. Genuine incremental improvement comes next. Scaling follows only after the conditions that produced the value are understood.

## The good-faith practitioner test

Assume practitioners are acting in what they reasonably believe to be the organisation’s interest.

Value can fragment because people have partial evidence, local incentives, limited authority, and no mechanism for resolving conflicts across boundaries. Describe the structural problem before attributing failure to behaviour or competence.

Leadership is responsible for shared commercial intent, guardrails, evidence, decision rights, and a route for conflicts that exceed a practitioner’s remit.

## The platform-role test

A platform can make decisions, dependencies, variants, evidence, and operating rules more visible and manageable. It can reduce friction and extend proven changes.

Tie each platform capability to the organisational tension it helps govern. Avoid feature catalogues and avoid implying that technology supplies judgement the organisation has not developed.

The platform provides an operating environment. Leadership and practitioners supply commercial intent and judgement.

## The executive-altitude test

Does the piece change how a leader frames the problem, or teach a practitioner how to execute the work?

For public thought leadership, favour diagnosis, distinctions, governing principles, and consequential questions. Remove complete diagnostic sequences, workshops, templates, scoring systems, and implementation instructions unless education is the explicit purpose.

## The conclusion test

A conclusion should compress, not inventory.

Remove questions that merely correspond to sections. Keep no more than two or three when questions are the right form. Each should carry a substantial part of the thesis.

A strong leadership question asks about a governing commercial condition, the structure supporting judgement, or the conditions under which an improvement can be extended.

## The literal-logic test

Good rhythm can hide weak logic.

Read polished lines literally. Check that the nouns and verbs can sustain the relationship claimed between them. Learning can be shared; a treatment can be scaled; harm may be reproduced as a consequence. Do not make unlike things parallel because the sentence sounds balanced.

Replace elegant ambiguity with precise language that still moves.

## The voice test

Read the draft aloud.

Watch for:

- consultancy distance;
- repeated slogan-like sentences;
- artificial one-line emphasis;
- excessive formality and expanded contractions;
- smooth phrases that the author would never say;
- edits that remove useful humour, frustration, or edge.

The final draft should sound clearer and better organised, while remaining recognisably the author’s.

## Completion gate

The edit is complete only when:

- every section has a distinct argumentative job;
- examples perform different work;
- repeated consequences have been compressed;
- contrast-by-negation is occasional rather than structural;
- local gains are judged against the governing commercial outcome;
- practitioner intent and leadership responsibility are proportionate;
- platform claims remain credible;
- the ending contains the thesis rather than a checklist;
- the most memorable lines survive literal scrutiny;
- the author’s voice remains recognisable.

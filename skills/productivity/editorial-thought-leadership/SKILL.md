---
name: editorial-thought-leadership
description: Edit essays, articles, and executive thought leadership for argumentative clarity, editorial discipline, logical precision, and a recognisable authorial voice. Use when a draft is repetitive, over-explained, example-heavy, dependent on contrast-by-negation, too tactical, or insufficiently tied to the governing commercial argument.
---

Edit the piece as an argument, not as a collection of locally polished paragraphs.

The goal is a draft that sounds like the author on a very good day: clearer, tighter, more logically exact, and more confident, without replacing the author with a generic executive voice.

Read any supplied voice guide before editing. Treat it as binding editorial context rather than optional style inspiration.

## 1. State the governing argument

Write one sentence that captures the claim the piece is trying to earn.

Then identify:

- the governing tension;
- the commercial or practical standard against which the argument is judged;
- the role of leadership, practitioners, technology, or institutions in the argument;
- the final change in perspective the reader should leave with.

Every major section must either establish, develop, complicate, or resolve that argument.

**Complete when:** the piece can be summarised as one coherent argument rather than a list of valid observations.

## 2. Build the argument map

For each section, write its job in a short clause. Mark passages that:

- advance the argument;
- repeat an earlier distinction;
- provide evidence or an example;
- qualify the claim;
- introduce a new implication;
- drift into method, checklist, or feature catalogue.

Merge or remove sections that perform the same job. Keep headings only where the thinking genuinely turns.

**Complete when:** every section has a distinct argumentative function and the headings form a readable summary of the piece.

## 3. Run the editorial pressure tests

Apply every relevant test in [`PRESSURE_TESTS.md`](PRESSURE_TESTS.md).

Pay particular attention to:

- repeated examples doing the same intellectual work;
- lists of consequences that should become one governing distinction;
- repeated defensive framing such as “it isn’t X, it is Y”;
- conclusions that recap the article as a checklist of questions;
- elegant phrases whose logic is weaker than their sound;
- technology claims that outrun the organisation’s ability to use the technology well;
- diagnoses that imply practitioner negligence where reasonable local decisions explain the outcome;
- local metrics presented without reference to the commercial result as a whole.

**Complete when:** every flagged passage has been cut, compressed, combined, or rewritten to advance the argument positively.

## 4. Protect authorial voice

Preserve the author’s actual opinions, rhythm, humour, scepticism, and degree of certainty.

Prefer spoken, considered prose with varied sentence length. Let connected thoughts remain connected. Use contractions where natural. Keep expressive lines when they still perform intellectual work.

Strengthen structure around a distinctive phrase before replacing it. Remove polish that makes the author less recognisable.

**Complete when:** the draft is more disciplined without sounding institutional, academic, Americanised, or consultancy-produced.

## 5. Preserve executive altitude

Keep public thought leadership centred on diagnosis and executive framing.

Publish the lens; retain the application. Expose the governing tension, consequential distinctions, and a small number of leadership questions. Remove workshop sequences, tactical prompts, scoring systems, implementation checklists, and exhaustive feature descriptions unless the user explicitly requests practical instruction.

When discussing a platform or product, connect capabilities to the organisational problem they make more governable. Present technology as an operating environment for judgement, not a substitute for strategy, leadership, evidence, or decision rights.

**Complete when:** the reader leaves seeing the problem differently, without receiving a substitute for the work required to solve it.

## 6. Edit the ending last

The ending should compress the argument rather than replay it.

Use prose by default. When questions genuinely improve the ending, keep no more than two or three. Each question must hold a substantial part of the thesis and operate at the level of leadership judgement.

A strong ending leaves the reader with:

- the commercial condition that must remain true;
- the leadership responsibility that makes good local judgement possible;
- the implication for scaling a proven improvement.

**Complete when:** the ending creates productive unease or a sharper governing question, not administrative homework.

## 7. Perform the final logic pass

Read every memorable line literally.

Check that:

- parallel phrases describe genuinely parallel things;
- causal claims are supported by the reasoning around them;
- metaphors clarify rather than conceal;
- a polished contrast has not replaced a positive claim;
- “learning”, “value”, “harm”, “scale”, “quality”, and similar abstractions have a clear referent;
- each conclusion follows from what the article has actually established.

Read the piece aloud. Repair any rhythm that sounds manufactured, slogan-heavy, or dependent on repeated rhetorical templates.

**Complete when:** the strongest-sounding lines also survive literal scrutiny.

## Output

Return the edited piece, followed by a concise editorial note covering only material structural decisions. Do not narrate every copy edit.
